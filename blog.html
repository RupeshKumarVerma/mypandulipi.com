The pandulipi journal

On manuscripts, scripture & the reasons behind the form

Six short reads on where the pandulipi tradition comes from, and why it still holds meaning in homes today.

01

रामचरितमानस की पाण्डुलिपि

How Goswami Tulsidas wrote the Ramcharitmanas in pandulipi form

Long before it was ever printed, the Ramcharitmanas was written by Goswami Tulsidas entirely by hand. He wrote it in Awadhi over many years, page by page, exactly the way we make our pandulipi today. There was no printing press and no machine-glued spine, just paper, ink, and a thread to hold the pages together. That handwritten form was not a small detail. It shaped how the text was meant to be read: slowly, page turned by hand, as an act of devotion, not something to rush through. When we make a Sundarkand in manuscript form today, we are not creating something new. We are simply bringing the text back closer to how its own author first wrote it.

02

हस्तलिपि की परम्परा

The tradition of hand-copying sacred texts in Sanatan culture

Long before printing existed anywhere in the world, Sanatan tradition already had a formal practice around copying scripture. Scribes and scholars would hand-copy granths as an act of seva in itself, not merely to create a new copy but because the act of writing scripture was believed to carry its own merit. Palm-leaf manuscripts, and later paper ones, were treated as objects to be preserved, wrapped, and passed down, never thrown away once the text was learned. This is part of why a manuscript-style book feels different in the hand than an ordinary paperback. It echoes a practice where the object itself, not just its words, was considered worthy of care.

03

शनिवार का महत्व

Why Sundarkand path is done on Saturdays

Saturday is traditionally linked with Shani Dev, and also, in most households, with Hanuman ji, whose worship is believed to ease the difficulties Shani’s influence can bring. The Sundarkand, being the kaand of the Ramcharitmanas most centred on Hanuman ji’s courage, devotion and strength, is naturally read on this day as both an offering to him and a source of protection for the reader. It is also simply the day most households and offices have time to gather people for a full path. Between the spiritual reason and the everyday convenience, Saturday became, over generations, the natural day. That is why you will find Sundarkand gatherings happening in some home or office almost every week.

04

हनुमान चालीसा का महत्व

The spiritual value of Hanuman Chalisa

Composed by Tulsidas as forty chaupais in praise of Hanuman ji, the Chalisa is one of the most widely recited texts in Sanatan practice, recited daily by many, not only on Saturdays. Part of its appeal is its length: short enough to recite in a few minutes, yet complete enough to cover Hanuman ji’s strength, humility, wisdom and devotion to Ram in a single sitting. For many households, it is the first text children learn by heart, and often the last one recited before sleep. Keeping a well-made copy near the mandir, one strong enough to survive years of daily use, is less about display and more about making sure the text is always within reach when it is needed.

05

ग्रंथ दान का पुण्य

Why gifting scripture is considered punya

In Indian tradition, some gifts matter more because of what they keep giving long after you hand them over. Vidya daan, the gift of knowledge, has always been seen as one of the most meaningful gifts a person can give. Gifting a granth follows the same idea. You are not giving something that gets used up and thrown away. You are giving something the person can come back to for years, something that may even shape how they raise their own children one day. This is part of why scripture has always been a favourite gift at weddings, griha pravesh, and naming ceremonies. A new beginning deserves a gift that lasts.

06

पुनरागमन

The quiet return of manuscript-style books into modern homes

For a couple of decades, most households moved toward whatever was cheapest and most convenient: mass-printed paperbacks, and more recently, scripture read straight off a phone screen. Both are useful, but neither asks anything of the reader the way a bound, thread-tied book does. What has changed recently is less about religion and more about a broader shift back toward things made with visible care, the same instinct that has brought back handmade pottery and hand-bound journals. Manuscript-style scripture is part of that same return: not a rejection of convenience, but a choice, for the texts that matter most, to keep one copy that is built to be kept.
